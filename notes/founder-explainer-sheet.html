A plain explanation

Why your product drifts, and what fixes it

No jargon, no code. This explains what a design system actually is, what problem it solves, and what it will ask of your team — so you can judge whether it's worth doing.

In one sentence

Every visual decision in your product gets written down once, in one place, and everything else reads from it — so your designs and your live product can't quietly drift apart.

You'll recognise this before you recognise the cause

Most teams don't notice the problem directly. They notice the symptoms, and treat each one as a separate annoyance. They're the same annoyance.

"It doesn't look like the design"

The mockup and the built version don't match. Someone eyeballs it, someone else re-checks it, and a week later it's off again somewhere new.

Small changes take surprisingly long

"Just darken the buttons" turns into a hunt through dozens of files, because the same colour was typed out by hand in each of them.

The product looks like several products

Three shades of your brand colour, four button sizes, spacing that shifts page to page. Nobody chose this. It accumulated.

New people take ages to get useful

There's no answer to "which blue do I use?" that doesn't involve asking someone. So everyone asks, or everyone guesses.

What a design system actually is

Think of it as your product's official colour chart — one that everything is wired to.

A paint manufacturer doesn't describe a colour as "that greeny blue." It has a name and a number, and every tin in every shop matches it. Nobody mixes their own.

A design system does the same for your product. Each decision — a colour, a text size, the space between things — gets a name and one agreed value. Your designers use the names. Your developers use the same names.

The important part isn't the list. It's the wiring: the names flow automatically from wherever they're defined into the code, so there's no step where a human copies a value across and gets it slightly wrong.

Where "the one place" lives depends on your team. If you have designers, it's the design file, and they own it. If you don't yet, it's a plain file of named values in your own repository, and it moves to the design file the day you hire someone to own it. The mechanism below is identical either way, which is the point: the wiring doesn't care who decides, only that one place does.

What it asks of your team

One habit, and it's the whole thing.

Visual decisions are made in the one agreed place, never typed straight into a screen. That's it. That's the discipline the entire system rests on.

It sounds trivial. It isn't — because in the moment, typing a colour directly is always faster. Ten seconds now, against a few minutes doing it properly. The reason products end up in a mess is that this trade looks obviously worth it every single time, and its cost only shows up months later, spread across a hundred small places.

So the system doesn't rely on everyone being disciplined. The automated checks catch it when they aren't — a value typed directly into the code fails the build and never reaches your customers. Good intentions are optional. That's the point.

What it won't do

Worth being straight about, because these get oversold and then disappoint.

It won't make your product well designed

It makes your product consistent. Consistently applying a poor decision is still a poor decision — the system enforces choices, it doesn't make them.

It won't remove the need for design judgement

Someone still decides what the values should be, and what a new screen should look like. This removes the copying, not the thinking.

It won't fix a team that won't use it

The checks stop bad values reaching customers, but if people route around the system, you'll get friction instead of drift. It needs a decision to adopt it, made once, out loud.

The part that matters commercially

  • •It keeps working without me. If we stop working together, nothing switches off and nothing expires.
  • •No subscription to a tool, no vendor holding your design decisions.
  • •Your developers don't have to learn a framework. It's built from the plainest possible parts on purpose.

Systems don't break. They erode.

Nothing here fails loudly. It slips — one hand-typed value, one skipped step — until the designs and the product describe two different things and nobody can say when that started. The point of the wiring is that the erosion becomes visible, early, and cheap to stop.
